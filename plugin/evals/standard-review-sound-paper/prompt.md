# Straw mulch and tomato yield in raised beds: a one-season block trial

A. Example and B. Sample, Invented Field Station. This paper is fictional: the study, the data and the authors were made up to test a review method.

## Abstract

We compared tomato yield in raised beds mulched with 10 cm of straw against unmulched beds in a randomized complete block trial with six blocks at one site in one season. Mulched plots yielded 4.10 kg per plant and unmulched plots 3.20 kg per plant, a difference of 0.90 kg per plant (95% confidence interval 0.59 to 1.21; paired t-test, p < 0.001), which is 28% of the unmulched yield. We did not measure soil moisture, weed cover or soil temperature, so the trial does not identify why mulch raised yield.

## Methods

The trial used twelve raised beds of 1.2 m by 2.4 m at one site, arranged in six blocks of two beds. Within each block one bed was assigned to mulch and one to no mulch by a coin toss. Each bed held eight plants of one cultivar, transplanted on the same day. Mulched beds received 10 cm of wheat straw one week after transplanting. All beds had the same drip irrigation schedule and the same fertilizer. Weeds were pulled from every bed once a week.

Fruit was picked twice a week for ten weeks and weighed per bed. Yield per plant is the bed total divided by eight. The bed is the unit of replication, so there are six independent pairs. The people picking and weighing fruit could see which beds were mulched. The difference between treatments was tested with a paired t-test across the six blocks. No beds or harvests were excluded.

Mulched beds yielded more than their unmulched partner in all six blocks. The mean difference was 0.90 kg per plant with a standard deviation of 0.30, giving a standard error of 0.12 and t = 7.4 with 5 degrees of freedom (p < 0.001). The 95% confidence interval for the difference is 0.59 to 1.21 kg per plant.

## Discussion

In this trial 10 cm of straw mulch increased tomato yield by 0.90 kg per plant, or 28%. The result is for one site, one cultivar and one season, and it may differ elsewhere. Mulch can affect soil moisture, weeds and soil temperature. We measured none of these, so we cannot say which of them produced the gain. Because the pickers were not blinded, a bias in picking or weighing cannot be excluded, although fruit was weighed on a scale and not judged by eye.
