# Claims of superconductivity

A claim of superconductivity asserts that a material, or a stated part of it, enters a state with zero direct-current resistance and expulsion of magnetic flux (the Meissner effect) below a transition temperature (Tc). A careful demonstration shows that this state belongs to the claimed phase and occupies a substantial part of the sample, and not only a filament, a surface layer or an impurity.

## 1. Zero resistance, not only a drop

**Question.** Does the resistance, measured with four terminals, fall to zero within a stated resolution, and does the paper give the residual value in ohms and as a fraction of the normal-state resistance, not only a drop or a kink?

**Why it matters.** A partial drop can come from a minority phase, a surface coating or a filament that superconducts while the rest of the sample does not. Resistance can reach zero along one connected path while bulk probes show no superconductivity, because transport can be dominated by narrow pathways. Structural and magnetic transitions also produce steps and kinks in resistance.

**Resolved by.** Four-terminal resistance through the transition on a linear scale and down to the noise floor, with the floor stated; the residual resistance compared with the normal-state value; the same result at more than one measuring current; zero resistance supported by a bulk probe on the same sample (items 2 and 3).

**Benchmark.** [1], [2]

## 2. Shielding fraction and flux expulsion

**Question.** Is the zero-field-cooled diamagnetic shielding given as a volume fraction, using a stated sample volume and demagnetising correction and a known field at the sample, and are field-cooled data shown alongside it and interpreted with pinning in mind?

**Why it matters.** A superconducting sheath or network of filaments can shield a mostly normal sample, so a shielding fraction of a few per cent or less does not show bulk superconductivity, and such small signals can disappear in fields of only tens of oersted. Because of demagnetisation, a finite sample shields more than its volume suggests, so an uncorrected fraction overstates the superconducting volume, most of all for thin plates in a perpendicular field. The magnet's remanent field can be tens of gauss, so a nominal zero-field cooling may take place in a field. Field-cooled expulsion is the stricter test of the Meissner effect, but pinning often limits it to a few per cent.

**Resolved by.** Zero-field-cooled and field-cooled magnetisation against temperature in a stated low field; the moment converted to volume susceptibility with the volume and the demagnetising factor stated; a shielding fraction close to full where bulk superconductivity is claimed; the field at the sample checked after a magnet reset or measured with a reference sample; the field dependence of the shielding shown.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [3], [4]

## 3. A bulk thermodynamic signature

**Question.** Is there a thermodynamic signature of the transition, such as an anomaly in heat capacity, and is its size compared with the normal-state electronic coefficient and with the superconducting volume fraction the paper claims?

**Why it matters.** Heat capacity probes the whole sample, whereas resistance can be dominated by narrow pathways, so a heat-capacity anomaly at the resistive Tc is strong evidence for bulk superconductivity. The absence of an anomaly is commonly traced to a small superconducting fraction. A large residual electronic term below Tc points to normal regions in the sample, and broad transitions need an entropy-conserving construction before the jump can be compared with expected values.

**Resolved by.** Heat capacity divided by temperature through Tc, in zero and applied fields; the jump normalised by the normal-state electronic coefficient, with the construction used for a broadened transition; the residual electronic term below Tc and the volume fraction it implies; an entropy balance between the normal and superconducting states; where no anomaly is resolved, the paper says so and limits the claim.

**Benchmark.** [1], [5]

## 4. Definition of Tc and agreement between probes

**Question.** Which criterion defines each quoted Tc (onset, midpoint or zero of resistance, onset of shielding, midpoint of the heat-capacity anomaly), and do the transitions seen by different probes on the same sample agree?

**Why it matters.** In a broad transition the onset of the resistance drop and the temperature of zero resistance can differ by many kelvin, and a drop at a high temperature can be followed by zero resistance and a very small shielding fraction only far below it. A claim of a new or higher Tc built on an onset criterion is not comparable with values defined by zero resistance or by a bulk probe.

**Resolved by.** The criterion stated for every Tc, with the transition width; resistive, magnetic and thermodynamic transitions measured on the same piece and compared; a headline Tc defined by zero resistance together with a bulk probe, or compared with earlier values defined in the same way.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [5]

## 5. Response to magnetic field and current

**Question.** Does the transition move to lower temperature in applied magnetic field in every probe used, is the criterion for any critical field or critical current stated, and is a critical field from resistance compared with one from a thermodynamic probe?

**Why it matters.** Suppression by field is the expected response of a superconducting transition, and a step that does not respond to field needs another explanation. Flux flow produces resistance inside the superconducting state, so a critical field taken from resistance tracks an irreversibility line that can differ from the thermodynamic upper critical field, and the result depends on the criterion. A critical current likewise depends on the voltage criterion used to define it.

**Resolved by.** Resistance against temperature at several fields, with the criterion for the critical field stated and the field dependence shown for more than one criterion where the transition broadens; the critical field compared with heat-capacity or magnetisation data; current-voltage curves with the critical-current criterion stated.

**Benchmark.** [2], [5]

## 6. Which phase superconducts

**Question.** Does the paper show that the claimed phase, and not an impurity, a flux residue or a contact material, carries the superconductivity, by quantifying phase purity and comparing the observed transition with those of known superconductors that could be present?

**Why it matters.** Many samples contain trace impurity phases, impurity phases precipitate once a dopant passes its solubility limit, and a minor superconducting phase or a filament can give zero resistance with only a small shielding signal. Crystals grown from a metal flux can hold inclusions of the flux and take some of it into the lattice, and any element or compound present in synthesis or mounting that superconducts can produce a transition of its own. The same composition can give filamentary superconductivity by one preparation route and bulk superconductivity by another.

**Resolved by.** Diffraction and composition analysis with detection limits stated; the observed Tc compared with those of elements, binaries and contact or flux materials present in synthesis and mounting; flux or surface layers removed, for example by etching or cleaving, with the transition unchanged; the superconducting volume fraction consistent with the fraction of the claimed phase; the same Tc from crystals or from a different synthesis route.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2]

## 7. Reproducibility across samples and conditions

**Question.** Do separate samples and synthesis batches show the transition, with what spread in Tc and superconducting fraction, are preparations that did not superconduct reported, and, for measurements under pressure or strain, are the pressure medium and the hydrostatic conditions stated?

**Why it matters.** Superconductivity in a new material can depend on the sample, and a result seen in one piece may come from a local region. Non-hydrostatic pressure can stabilise islands of another phase that give filamentary superconductivity, and shear strain in a solidified pressure medium can make results non-reproducible. Unsuccessful preparations are rarely reported, which hides how often a transition actually appears.

**Resolved by.** The number of samples and batches stated, with Tc and superconducting fraction for each; preparations that did not superconduct reported; for high-pressure work, the medium, the pressure calibration and the degree of hydrostaticity; the result reproduced on samples from separate syntheses.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2]
