# Thin-film growth and epitaxy

Films grown by molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), pulsed laser deposition (PLD), sputtering, chemical vapour deposition (CVD) or atomic layer deposition (ALD) are used to claim new phases, strain-tuned or interface-driven properties and thickness-dependent behaviour. Each claim rests on knowing the film's thickness, composition, strain state and interfaces, and on separating the film from the substrate it was grown on.

## 1. Growth conditions and substrate temperature

**Question.** Does the paper give the conditions needed to repeat the growth: substrate and its preparation, substrate temperature and how it was measured, background gas and pressure, laser fluence and spot size or source fluxes, deposition rate, target-to-substrate distance, and the cooling and any post-growth anneal?

**Why it matters.** Film properties depend strongly on these parameters, and settings that work in one chamber do not necessarily give the same film in another. The heater reading is not the substrate surface temperature, which depends on thermal contact with the holder; a pyrometer reading depends on an emissivity that can change during growth, and the temperature can vary by up to about 100 °C across a few millimetres.

**Resolved by.** The growth parameters listed for each film in the claims, including buffer and capping layers; how and where the substrate temperature was measured or calibrated, and how the substrate was mounted; the cooling atmosphere and any anneal; the key property shown in films from separate growth runs, not only in pieces of one film.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2]

## 2. Thickness and growth-rate calibration

**Question.** How was the film thickness determined (for example by counting reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) oscillations, X-ray reflectivity, thickness fringes in diffraction, a quartz-crystal monitor or a separate calibration film), and was it measured on the films whose properties are reported?

**Why it matters.** Thickness enters every quantity normalised by volume or area and every claim of thickness-dependent behaviour. Counting RHEED oscillations assumes one period per layer, which holds for layer-by-layer growth, and becomes impractical as the signal decays in thicker films; the period measures the growth rate only over a limited range of conditions and orientations. A thickness from X-ray reflectivity depends on the fitted model as well as on the measurement.

**Resolved by.** Thickness measured on the reported films by a stated method, preferably two methods that agree; for counted layers, the RHEED trace shown over the whole growth; for reflectivity, the data shown with the fit, the layer model and the intensity range measured; for thicknesses derived from a rate calibration, when that calibration was made and how stable the rate was.

**Benchmark.** [1], [4], [5]

## 3. What in situ monitoring shows

**Question.** Where RHEED or another in situ probe is used to support claims of layer-by-layer growth, flat surfaces or high-quality epitaxy, does the paper show the patterns and intensity traces with the diffraction conditions, and is each claim confirmed by an ex situ measurement?

**Why it matters.** RHEED uses grazing incidence and probes only the topmost layers. Sustained intensity oscillations indicate layer-by-layer growth and damped ones a mixed or island growth mode, but the intensity also depends on surface morphology and on the geometry of the instrument, and under some flux conditions and on vicinal surfaces the oscillations do not track the deposited layers directly. A sharp pattern describes the surface and does not by itself show the phase content of the whole film or the state of the buried interface.

**Resolved by.** RHEED patterns before, during and after growth, with intensity traces and the beam azimuth and incidence stated; surface flatness and growth mode confirmed by atomic force microscopy or X-ray methods; phase purity and interface quality established by ex situ diffraction or microscopy, not by RHEED alone.

**Benchmark.** [1], [4]

## 4. Stoichiometry transfer and composition

**Question.** Was the composition of the film, including oxygen or other volatile constituents, measured on the film by a method suited to a thin layer on a substrate, or is it assumed from the target or from the nominal fluxes?

**Why it matters.** In PLD the film composition can depart from the target's: laser fluence, spot size and background pressure change how strongly light elements are scattered in the plume or lost from the growing surface, and fluence alone can change the lattice constant and defect structure of the film. In sputtering, energetic particles from the target and the plasma, mainly at low working pressure, can resputter the growing film selectively and damage it. In MBE the composition is only as good as the flux calibration unless the growth has a self-regulating (adsorption-controlled) window, and cation off-stoichiometry of a few percent can change properties. Dilute oxygen vacancies can change properties strongly and are often hard to detect.

**Resolved by.** Cation composition measured on the reported films, for example by Rutherford backscattering spectrometry or elastic recoil detection, with its uncertainty; for MBE, the flux calibration method and its accuracy, or evidence of an adsorption-controlled window; for sputtering, the working pressure and target-substrate geometry, with the composition measured on films grown under those conditions; for oxygen-sensitive materials, the oxygen pressure during growth and cooling stated and the effect of a post-anneal tested; the claimed property checked across films grown at different fluence, flux ratio or working pressure.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [3], [12], [13], [14]

## 5. Strain state and relaxation

**Question.** Is the strain state measured, with both in-plane and out-of-plane lattice parameters, for the films on which strain-related claims rest, or is it inferred from the nominal mismatch and the film thickness?

**Why it matters.** A film stays coherent with the substrate only up to a critical thickness, beyond which misfit dislocations relax it. Metastable layers can remain coherent well beyond the equilibrium critical thickness, and the thickness at which relaxation is observed depends on growth kinetics and on instrumental resolution. An out-of-plane lattice parameter alone does not fix the strain: it follows the in-plane strain through the Poisson ratio and also shifts with non-stoichiometry and defects.

**Resolved by.** In-plane and out-of-plane lattice parameters measured for the films concerned, for example from reciprocal-space maps; the degree of relaxation given as a function of thickness; the relaxed reference lattice parameter stated with its source; for comparisons between substrates, evidence that composition and defect density did not change with the substrate, for example films grown side by side.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [6]

## 6. Interface abruptness and interdiffusion

**Question.** Where a claim rests on a sharp interface or on a layer only a few unit cells thick, does the paper measure the chemical width of the interface and any intermixing between film and substrate or between layers?

**Why it matters.** Cations can intermix over the first few unit cells across an interface during growth, and intermixed cations can act as dopants in the neighbouring layer. The extent depends on the substrate, its surface termination and the growth conditions. Detecting it needs chemically sensitive probes, since growth monitoring sees only the surface.

**Resolved by.** Element-resolved profiles across the relevant interface, for example from electron energy-loss spectroscopy, medium-energy ion scattering, secondary-ion mass spectrometry or surface X-ray diffraction; substrate preparation and termination described; the measured interface width compared with the length scale of the claimed effect.

**Benchmark.** [1], [7]

## 7. Substrate and buffer contributions

**Question.** Does the paper show that the measured signal comes from the film and not from the substrate, the buffer layer or a substrate changed by the growth, for example by measuring a bare substrate taken through the same heating, atmosphere and cooling without deposition?

**Why it matters.** Growth at high temperature and low oxygen pressure can reduce an oxide substrate and make it conducting through its bulk, which can be mistaken for conduction in the film or at the interface; annealing in oxygen and depth-resolved measurements have been needed to separate the two. In bulk-sensitive measurements a thin film is a small fraction of the total signal, and substrates can contribute responses that are not simple to subtract.

**Resolved by.** A bare substrate, or substrate and buffer alone, processed under the same thermal and gas conditions and measured in the same way; the effect of a post-anneal in an oxidising atmosphere on the claimed property; measurements showing that the signal scales with film thickness or is confined to the film; the substrate signal shown alongside the film signal.

**Benchmark.** [7], [8], [12]

## 8. Self-limiting growth in atomic layer deposition

**Question.** For films grown by ALD, does the paper show that each half-reaction saturates (growth per cycle against precursor dose and purge time), give the growth per cycle and the temperature at which it holds, and show thickness against the number of cycles from the start of growth?

**Why it matters.** The thickness control and conformality claimed for ALD rest on self-terminating surface reactions, so they are established only when saturation is shown. The growth per cycle depends on temperature and can differ in the early cycles, where substrate-enhanced or substrate-inhibited growth and island formation mean that the thickness of a very thin film is not the number of cycles times the steady growth per cycle. Conformality depends on the structure coated and has to be measured on defined three-dimensional structures.

**Resolved by.** Saturation curves for each precursor and purge; growth per cycle against temperature; thickness against cycle number including the first cycles, with the point at which the film becomes continuous; for conformality claims, the test structure described by its aspect ratio and the thickness profile measured along it.

**Benchmark.** [9], [10], [11]
