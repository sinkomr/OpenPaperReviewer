# Claims of new magnetic, ferroic or exotic ground states

These claims assert that a material settles at low temperature into a state with a particular broken symmetry, or, for a quantum spin liquid (QSL), into a correlated state without conventional order. They cover magnetic order, spin glasses, ferroelectric and multiferroic order, charge density waves (CDW) and spin density waves (SDW), and nematic states that break rotational but not translational symmetry, and each has to be told apart from disorder, impurity phases, spin freezing and conventional order.

## 1. Long-range order or freezing

**Question.** What evidence shows that the claimed order is static, long-range and a property of the bulk, and not short-range correlations or a freezing of spins or dipoles on the time scale of the probe?

**Why it matters.** Freezing also gives a cusp in the susceptibility, a split between zero-field-cooled and field-cooled magnetisation and static local fields, and the same split appears below the transition of an ordinary ferromagnet with defects. In a glass the peak of the ac susceptibility moves to lower temperature at lower frequency, because it marks freezing on the time scale of the measurement. Local, quasi-static probes also detect short-range or fluctuating order once disorder or boundaries pin it.

**Resolved by.** The ac susceptibility at several frequencies, with any shift of the peak quantified; sharp, resolution-limited magnetic or superlattice peaks in diffraction, with the correlation length stated; ageing and memory tests where a glassy state is claimed; the time scale of each probe stated, and fast and slow probes compared before a static order is claimed.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [3]

## 2. The order belongs to the main phase

**Question.** How does the paper show that the signal of order comes from the main phase and from most of the sample, and not from a secondary phase, precipitates, clusters of dopant atoms, contamination, a surface layer or a small fraction of the sites?

**Why it matters.** Small amounts of a secondary phase, including nanoscale precipitates and aggregates of dopant atoms, can escape standard laboratory diffraction and yet order at higher temperatures than the host, so their transition is read as a transition of the host. A small subset of moments, such as impurity spins on mixed or defective sites, can dominate the low-temperature response of the whole sample.

**Resolved by.** Phase purity checked with methods sensitive to small and nanoscale inclusions, such as high-intensity diffraction, electron microscopy with element mapping and element-specific spectroscopy; the transition temperature compared with those of plausible secondary phases; the ordered moment or polarisation per formula unit, or the ordered volume fraction, compared with what the claim requires; the result reproduced in samples from different growths.

**Benchmark.** [4], [5], [6]

## 3. Absence of order is not enough

**Question.** For a claimed quantum spin liquid or other disordered quantum state, how far below the interaction scale do the measurements reach, and what positive evidence, beyond the absence of order, supports the specific state claimed?

**Why it matters.** The usual first test is the absence of order at temperatures well below the interaction scale, often expressed as the ratio of the Curie-Weiss temperature to any ordering or freezing temperature. This negative criterion also admits ordinary quantum paramagnets and valence bond solids, whose symmetry breaking can be weak and hidden by disorder. No single measurement identifies a spin liquid, so the case rests on several probes that agree.

**Resolved by.** The Curie-Weiss fit range and the resulting moment and temperature stated, with the frustration ratio; measurements extending well below the interaction scale with probes sensitive to small or slowly fluctuating moments; dimer or valence bond order looked for and excluded; predictions specific to the claimed state, for example for the excitation spectrum, thermal transport or field dependence, tested and shown to differ from those of a conventional paramagnet.

**Benchmark.** [6], [7]

## 4. Disorder that mimics the claimed state

**Question.** Does the paper quantify structural disorder, such as site mixing, vacancies, stacking faults and bond randomness, and test whether disorder alone accounts for the features offered as evidence of an exotic state?

**Why it matters.** Quenched disorder in a frustrated magnet can leave a random network of weakly coupled moments that gives power-law specific heat and susceptibility and no order, and the response of these few moments can outweigh that of the bulk. Impurity spins from mixed site occupancy can dominate the low-energy spectrum, and the actual level of disorder can exceed what the nominal composition suggests.

**Resolved by.** Site occupancies and defect densities refined or measured and reported; samples with different levels of disorder compared; low-temperature specific heat and magnetisation tested against the scaling expected from random moments, such as collapse onto a function of temperature divided by field; the impurity contribution estimated, for example with a local probe that separates defect and bulk signals, and the effect of removing it shown.

**Benchmark.** [5], [6], [7]

## 5. Ferroelectric switching

**Question.** For a claimed ferroelectric state, does the paper show a spontaneous polarisation that an electric field reverses, with electrostatic, charge-injection and electrochemical contributions separated from the switching signal?

**Why it matters.** Hysteresis loops and remanent polar contrast in local piezoresponse measurements are reported in many materials that are not ferroelectric in bulk, or in which size effects should suppress ferroelectricity. Electrostatic forces, injected charge and ionic motion give similar loops and contrast, so hysteresis alone does not establish polar order.

**Resolved by.** Where the sample allows, a macroscopic measurement of polarisation against field, with non-switching contributions separated from the switching signal; for local probes, the dependence of the loops on the amplitude and frequency of the ac drive, the sense in which each loop is traversed, and measurements that separate electrostatic from electromechanical response, such as contact Kelvin probe force microscopy; structural evidence for a polar symmetry.

**Benchmark.** [8]

## 6. Dielectric and magnetoelectric signatures

**Question.** Where a dielectric anomaly, a very large permittivity or a magnetocapacitance is offered as evidence of ferroic order or of magnetoelectric coupling, has the paper excluded contributions from electrodes, grain boundaries and magnetoresistance?

**Why it matters.** Depletion layers at contacts and grain boundaries give Maxwell-Wagner relaxations, which produce apparently very large permittivities and a step-like frequency dependence that resemble intrinsic dipolar relaxation. Combined with magnetoresistance, the same effect gives a magnetocapacitance without any magnetoelectric coupling, so a magnetocapacitance alone does not show that a material is multiferroic.

**Resolved by.** Dielectric spectra over a wide frequency range analysed with an equivalent circuit; different sample thicknesses, electrode materials and contact configurations compared; resistivity and magnetoresistance measured on the same samples; magnetoelectric coupling shown directly, for example as a polarisation changed by a magnetic field or a magnetisation changed by an electric field.

**Benchmark.** [9], [10]

## 7. Density waves

**Question.** For a claimed charge or spin density wave, does the paper show a static, long-range modulation in the bulk with its own transition, and does it distinguish the modulation from quasiparticle interference, defect-induced oscillations, surface reconstruction and a structural distortion of the lattice?

**Why it matters.** Surface probes see modulations near defects and quasiparticle interference patterns whose wave vectors can lie close to an expected ordering vector. Order gives peaks at fixed wave vectors with a coherent phase, interference gives features that disperse with energy, and the two are not always easy to separate. Fermi-surface nesting is often absent at the observed wave vector or stronger at another one, so a nesting argument alone does not establish an electronically driven density wave, and the line between a density wave and a lattice transition is not sharp.

**Resolved by.** Bulk diffraction showing superlattice peaks, with their correlation length and temperature dependence; for local probes, a wave vector shown to be independent of energy over a range of energies, and the phase of the modulation analysed; the accompanying lattice distortion measured; any claim about the mechanism supported by measured electron-phonon coupling or a calculated electronic susceptibility, not by the shape of the Fermi surface alone.

**Benchmark.** [3], [11], [12]

## 8. Broken rotational symmetry

**Question.** For a claimed nematic or other rotational-symmetry-breaking state, does the paper show that the anisotropy arises spontaneously, and not from strain, the substrate, the crystal structure, twinning or the measurement geometry?

**Why it matters.** Domains of opposite orientation average a spontaneous anisotropy to zero in bulk measurements, while the stress or crystal anisotropy that aligns the domains is itself a symmetry-breaking field and blurs the transition. Most tests therefore rest on an electronic anisotropy that is much larger and more strongly temperature dependent than a small symmetry-breaking field would produce. Because structural, orbital and spin orders of the same symmetry induce one another, measurements in the ordered state alone cannot show which one drives the transition.

**Resolved by.** The lattice distortion or applied strain stated and compared with the size of the electronic anisotropy; the anisotropy measured against a controlled strain or field, with a response that grows on approach to the transition from above; domains imaged or their population controlled; the measurement geometry rotated or contacts exchanged to exclude geometric effects.

**Benchmark.** [3], [13]
