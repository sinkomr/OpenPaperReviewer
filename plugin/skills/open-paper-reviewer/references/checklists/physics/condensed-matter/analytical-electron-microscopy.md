# Analytical electron microscopy

Electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, also written EDS), recorded in the transmission electron microscope (TEM), the scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) or the scanning electron microscope (SEM), are used to claim local composition, where elements sit in a specimen down to single atomic columns, and whether an element is present at all. The shape and position of EELS edges are also used to claim bonding, valence and coordination.

## 1. Specimen thickness, geometry and absorption

**Question.** Is the thickness of the analysed region reported (for EELS, as t/λ from the low-loss spectrum of the same area), and for EDX are the specimen tilt, the detector and holder geometry and, in the SEM, the surface finish stated and taken into account?

**Why it matters.** In EELS the background under an edge rises steeply with thickness and plural scattering changes its shape, so an apparent signal can vary with thickness alone; converting t/λ to an absolute thickness needs an inelastic mean free path, and a widely used parameterised value has been found to differ from a measured one by nearly 20 %. In EDX the measured line ratios depend on X-ray absorption on the way to the detector, which changes with thickness, tilt and, in the SEM, surface topography, and some holders shadow the detectors at some tilts.

**Resolved by.** A t/λ value or map from the same area, with analysis restricted to thin regions or spectra deconvolved to the single-scattering distribution; the mean free path and its source stated wherever an absolute thickness or areal density is quoted; for TEM-EDX, an absorption correction or a shown thin-film limit, and a tilt and holder at which no detector is shadowed; for quantitative SEM-EDX, flat, polished and unetched specimens, or a statement that rough or particulate specimens give only semi-quantitative results.

**Benchmark.** [3], [4], [5], [7], [9], [10]

## 2. Peak and edge identification and background

**Question.** Is every element assignment checked against overlapping lines and artefact peaks, and for EELS are the background model and the fitting and integration windows stated, with a background-subtracted spectrum shown?

**Why it matters.** Automatic EDX peak identification mislabels a few per cent of major-constituent peaks and more of the minor and trace ones, mostly where lines of different elements lie close in energy. At high count rates, coincidence (sum) peaks appear at energies that can match apparent trace elements. EELS edges sit on a large background, so an error in extrapolating it passes directly into the extracted signal and grows with distance from the fitting window, and at low counts per channel the usual power-law fit can be biased.

**Resolved by.** Representative spectra shown with each assigned peak or edge labelled; overlaps resolved by peak fitting with the residual spectrum inspected, or confirmed with a second line family or beam energy; detector dead time kept low, about 10 % or less, or coincidence corrected, when trace elements are claimed; for EELS, raw and background-subtracted spectra with the pre-edge residual scattering about zero, windows given, and model or reference-spectrum fitting where edges overlap.

**Benchmark.** [2], [5], [7]

## 3. Quantification and detection limits

**Question.** Where compositions, ratios, areal densities or the absence of an element are reported, does the paper state the quantification method (measured standards, k-factors or ζ-factors, or calculated cross-sections) with the acquisition conditions it depends on, an uncertainty and, for trace or absence claims, a detection limit?

**Why it matters.** EDX quantification without locally measured standards ("standardless") has much wider errors than standards-based analysis, and it normalises the total to 100 %, which hides missing elements and geometry errors. Thin-film EDX factors depend on standards and on absorption, and EELS intensities convert to numbers of atoms only through cross-sections defined for the collection angle and energy window used. Light elements such as oxygen are often calculated from an assumed stoichiometry, not measured, and whether an element can be detected depends on dose, background and cross-section, so a missing peak or edge does not by itself show that the element is absent.

**Resolved by.** The method named with its standards or calculated factors; beam energy, collection angle and energy windows given; the procedure checked on a material of known composition measured in the same way; the un-normalised analytical total reported for standards-based EDX; light-element values marked as measured or calculated; an uncertainty that covers more than counting statistics; for trace or absence claims, a minimum detectable amount for the dose and thickness used.

**Benchmark.** [3], [5], [7], [9]

## 4. Signals that do not come from the analysed feature

**Question.** Does the paper show that the elements on which a claim rests come from the feature analysed, and not from the support grid, the holder, the microscope, a coating, contamination or a surface layer?

**Why it matters.** Scattered electrons and X-rays excite the grid, holder and column parts, so system and spurious X-ray peaks appear in TEM spectra, and a lamella cut by focused ion beam is much smaller than the grid that carries it. An element that is also present in the grid or holder cannot be assigned to the specimen from the raw spectrum. Conductive coatings, native oxide or reaction layers and carbon contamination built up during long acquisitions add signal of their own, and at low beam energy a surface layer can be a large part of the volume sampled.

**Resolved by.** A spectrum recorded through a hole or from the bare support under the same conditions; grid and holder materials stated and chosen to avoid the elements of interest; specimen tilt kept low where possible; coating material and thickness stated and matched between specimen and standards; surface layers ruled out or accounted for; spectra from the surrounding matrix, support or substrate shown for comparison.

**Benchmark.** [1], [7], [8], [11]

## 5. Spatial origin of the signal

**Question.** When a signal is assigned to a particular layer, interface, particle or atomic column, does the paper compare the size of that feature with the volume that actually produced the signal, and not only with the probe size?

**Why it matters.** In the SEM the electron range grows steeply with beam energy, roughly as its 1.66th power, and quantification assumes that the excited volume has a uniform composition. In the TEM the signal comes from the whole column of material along the beam and from a region that widens with thickness, while low-loss EELS signals are delocalised well beyond the probe. At atomic resolution, with a crystal viewed along a zone axis, channelling makes the X-ray signal a non-linear function of column composition and draws signal from neighbouring columns, increasingly so in thicker specimens.

**Resolved by.** Beam energy and specimen thickness reported with an estimate of the volume sampled; for SEM-EDX of small features or thin layers, a lower beam energy or a thinned specimen; for interfaces and thin layers, a simulation of the expected profile that includes beam broadening, projection and neighbouring layers; for compositions of atomic columns, simulations that include channelling, or integration over unit cells or a small tilt, with the loss of resolution stated.

**Benchmark.** [6], [8], [12]

## 6. Denoising and multivariate analysis of maps

**Question.** If spectrum images were denoised, decomposed or locally averaged, for example by principal component analysis (PCA), is the feature on which the claim rests also visible in unprocessed or simply summed spectra, and are the pre-treatment and the number of retained components stated?

**Why it matters.** Keeping too few components removes weak or local signals. In EELS, where the background dominates the variance, PCA of raw spectra can shift fine-structure peaks by up to about an electronvolt and produce map contrast that follows a neighbouring edge. In EDX, PCA of sparse, unweighted data can blur thin layers together or make one element appear in place of another. The number and shape of the components need not match the physical phases present, and local averaging of the background creates artefacts at abrupt boundaries.

**Resolved by.** Raw or summed spectra from the relevant pixels shown next to processed maps; for EELS, the background removed before any decomposition; for EDX, the weighting for counting noise and any binning or smoothing used to reduce sparsity stated; the truncation criterion stated; residuals examined for spatial structure; an averaging width smaller than the features interpreted.

**Benchmark.** [2], [13]

## 7. Fine structure and valence assignments

**Question.** Where valence, coordination or bonding is inferred from the shape or position of an EELS edge, is the assignment tied to reference spectra or calculations for that edge, and is the energy scale shown to be stable to well within the shifts interpreted?

**Why it matters.** Near-edge structure responds to valence and also to coordination, site symmetry, distortion and spin state, so a change in edge shape is not a unique signature of valence. The shift between whole valence states is of the order of an electronvolt or two, so a fractional change in valence corresponds to a shift comparable to residual energy drift and calibration error, and to the shifts that denoising can introduce; the beam can itself change the valence being measured.

**Resolved by.** Reference spectra from compounds of known valence and coordination, or calculated spectra, compared with the data for the same edge; the energy scale calibrated against a reference edge of known energy, with drift corrected or bounded; unprocessed spectra shown alongside any filtered ones; a check that the feature is stable under the beam (item 8).

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [4], [6]

## 8. Radiation damage

**Question.** Is the electron fluence (dose) on the analysed area reported, and is there a check that the composition and the spectral feature used for the claim did not change during acquisition?

**Why it matters.** The beam can remove some elements preferentially, which changes the composition measured by EDX or EELS, and it can change bonding and valence; in some oxides the fine structure changes at fluences where images still show an intact structure. Focused probes and long acquisitions for weak signals deliver the highest fluences, and the damage mechanism and its threshold depend on the material and on the beam energy.

**Resolved by.** Beam energy, probe current, dwell or exposure time and the resulting fluence stated; a time or fluence series of the feature used for the claim, or a measured critical fluence, with the reported data taken below it; spectra from the start and the end of the acquisition compared; lower-dose, lower-voltage or cryogenic conditions where the material requires them.

**Benchmark.** [3], [4]
