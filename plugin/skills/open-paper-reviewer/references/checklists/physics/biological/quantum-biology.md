# Claims of quantum effects in biology

## 1. Electronic or vibrational coherence

**Question.** When oscillations in time-resolved spectra are presented as electronic coherence, how does the paper exclude vibrational and vibronic origins, and what evidence besides the beat frequency supports the assignment?

**Why it matters.** Laser pulses broad enough to cover several electronic states also excite molecular vibrations impulsively, and these give long-lived beats of their own, in the ground state as well as the excited state. Assigning beats from their frequency, or from frequency and phase, does not separate electronic, vibrational and mixed vibronic coherence.

**Resolved by.** Beat frequencies compared with independently measured vibrational frequencies; an analysis of where each beat appears in rephasing and non-rephasing maps, or polarisation-controlled measurements, compared with model vibrational and excitonic systems; the dephasing time of each component stated; where possible, a perturbation that shifts the electronic energy gaps, such as a mutation, tested for a matching shift in beat frequency.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2]

## 2. Coherence lifetime against the timescale of function

**Question.** Does the paper compare the lifetime of the claimed coherence, measured or modelled under the conditions in which the process operates, with the timescale of the biological step it is said to influence?

**Why it matters.** A coherence can affect a transfer, reaction or sensing step only if it survives for a comparable time. Interexciton coherences in photosynthetic complexes decay within about 100 fs at room temperature, much faster than transfer between complexes, whereas a radical pair must keep its spin coherence for close to a microsecond, about one electron Larmor period, to respond to an Earth-strength field. Models that assume negligible relaxation are rarely realistic.

**Resolved by.** The coherence or spin relaxation time stated at physiological temperature, with how it was obtained; a direct comparison with the rate of the step in question; for spin-based claims, the relaxation rates used in the model justified for the molecular environment, and the result shown for a range of plausible rates.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [5]

## 3. Quantum, not only coherent

**Question.** Does the paper state what makes the claimed effect non-classical, and does it show that a classical or incoherent model with the same independently determined parameters fails to reproduce the observations?

**Why it matters.** Coherence is not specific to quantum mechanics: classical oscillators show it, and models that treat the electronic excitation classically, or describe transfer as incoherent hopping between partly delocalised states, reproduce many spectroscopic and transfer observations. Needing quantum chemistry to compute an energy surface, or fitting a model that contains quantum terms, does not show that quantum dynamics governs the outcome.

**Resolved by.** An explicit definition of the quantum feature claimed, such as interexciton coherence, tunnelling beyond semiclassical behaviour or entangled spin states; a quantitative comparison with an incoherent or semiclassical baseline, such as Förster hopping, transition-state theory with zero-point corrections or classical electron-transfer theory; the difference between the models compared with the uncertainty of the data.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [7]

## 4. From laboratory conditions to the living organism

**Question.** Were the excitation source, temperature and sample state under which the effect was observed those of the biological process, and where the evidence comes from isolated proteins, receptor assays in cultured cells, synthetic model compounds or simulations, does the paper show that the same molecule, mechanism and conditions operate in the living organism, or argue explicitly how the result carries over?

**Why it matters.** Ultrafast experiments excite with short, coherent laser pulses, whereas organisms absorb weak, continuous, incoherent sunlight, and whether natural light creates the same coherent dynamics is disputed. Many observations of long-lived beats were made on isolated complexes at cryogenic temperatures, where dephasing is slower than at physiological temperature. A result on an isolated complex or protein leaves open whether that molecule is present, suitably arranged and involved in the intact organism, and whether energy or signals actually pass through it there. Behavioural and receptor-level tests of the same hypothesis can disagree, because steps outside the receptor and the cellular context are missing from in vitro assays.

**Resolved by.** The temperature, excitation conditions and sample preparation stated for each data set; measurements at physiological temperature; for light-driven processes, an argument or model that addresses incoherent excitation; the molecule identified in the relevant tissue; a genetic or other perturbation in vivo that changes the response as predicted; parameters from in vitro work shown to be consistent with the in vivo response; conclusions limited to the conditions actually measured, with what was shown in vitro separated from what is inferred for the organism.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [3], [6], [7]

## 5. What the isotope effect tests

**Question.** When an isotope effect is offered as evidence, for example a KIE for tunnelling, a deuterated odorant for vibrational sensing or an isotope change for spin dynamics, does the paper show that the effect belongs to the claimed step, separate it from other consequences of the substitution, and use more than its size?

**Why it matters.** Binding, conformational changes and product release can mask the intrinsic KIE of the chemical step, and an inflated KIE is only one of several tunnelling diagnostics, alongside its temperature dependence, the ratio of Arrhenius prefactors and relations between hydrogen, deuterium and tritium effects. Isotope substitution also changes zero-point energies, hydrogen bonding, binding and solubility, and labelled compounds can carry trace impurities that drive a response. Isotopes that differ in nuclear spin test a hyperfine-dependent spin mechanism in a way that mass changes alone do not.

**Resolved by.** Intrinsic KIEs extracted, or commitments to catalysis shown to be negligible; KIEs measured over a temperature range, with prefactor ratios compared with the semiclassical range; the chemical and isotopic purity of labelled compounds checked after purification; substitutions that change different properties, such as nuclear spin and mass, compared with each other.

**Benchmark.** [4], [6], [8]

## 6. Magnetic-field effects

**Question.** For a claimed magnetic-field effect on a biological process or on behaviour, does the paper characterise the static and oscillating fields at the sample, use field strengths relevant to the claim, and show a dependence on field strength, direction or frequency that matches the proposed spin mechanism?

**Why it matters.** Effects seen at millitesla fields in chemical or protein systems do not transfer directly to Earth-strength fields of about 50 µT, where the predicted changes in reaction yield are small and need long spin coherence. Many reported biological effects of weak low-frequency and radiofrequency fields have not been independently replicated, and a field reported only as power density or specific absorption rate, or ambient electromagnetic noise and field inhomogeneity, leaves the field at the sample unknown.

**Resolved by.** The magnetic field at the sample position measured, including ambient noise and gradients; the response measured over a range of field strengths and, for oscillating fields, frequencies, and compared with predictions from the hyperfine couplings of the proposed radical pair; the predicted size of the yield change stated next to the measured effect; effects at Earth-strength fields shown directly, not extrapolated from strong fields.

**Benchmark.** [2], [5], [8]

## 7. From signature to biological function

**Question.** Does the paper show that the quantum effect changes a biological output, such as yield, rate, selectivity or behaviour, relative to an appropriate reference, and not only that a quantum signature is present?

**Why it matters.** A process can involve tunnelling or coherence without these explaining its performance. For enzymes the relevant comparison is with the same reaction in solution, where tunnelling also occurs, and model calculations of coherence-assisted energy transfer have predicted gains of only a few per cent over incoherent transfer. A functional role means that the quantum effect achieves something more efficiently than a classical mechanism, or achieves what it could not.

**Resolved by.** A quantitative estimate of how much the effect contributes to the biological output, against the uncatalysed reaction or the incoherent counterpart; a perturbation that changes the quantum feature, such as couplings, isotopes or fields, with the measured change in function compared in direction and size with the prediction.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [7]
