# Claims of indirect dark matter signals

## 1. Model of the conventional emission

**Question.** How was the astrophysical emission in the target modelled (for gamma rays, emission from interstellar gas, inverse Compton scattering, large-scale structures and the isotropic background; for X-rays, the thermal plasma; for cosmic rays, secondary production), and do the excess, its spectrum and its spatial shape persist across the plausible range of those models, not only in one?

**Why it matters.** The places where a DM signal should be brightest also have the brightest astrophysical emission, and that emission is predicted from distributions of gas, starlight and cosmic rays that are not well measured in three dimensions. The spectrum of a residual excess can depend strongly on the background model chosen, no finite set of models covers all the uncertainty, and some public background models absorb large-scale residuals by construction.

**Resolved by.** Each background component listed with its inputs; a set of alternative models that vary the gas maps, cosmic-ray source distributions and other inputs, with the amplitude and spectrum of the excess shown for each; background models chosen to suit a diffuse analysis, not only point-source work; residual maps after the fit; the spread between models carried into the quoted significance and parameters.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2]

## 2. Unresolved astrophysical sources

**Question.** Could a population of individually undetected astrophysical sources with a similar spectrum produce the excess, and does the paper test this, for example through the statistics of photon counts per pixel, the implied number and brightness of sources, or searches at other wavelengths?

**Why it matters.** Some known source classes, such as rotating neutron stars, emit spectra close to those expected from DM annihilation. Many faint sources and a smooth DM signal can give the same mean flux but different pixel-to-pixel statistics, and a fit that admits only smooth components assigns emission from faint sources to the smooth template. Excesses of charged antiparticles also have candidate astrophysical sources, such as pulsar wind nebulae.

**Resolved by.** A fit that allows both smooth and point-source components, with the source-count distribution fitted and the result shown under more than one background model; the required source population compared with known populations in number and luminosity; predictions for radio or X-ray observations of such a population; for charged particles, nearby astrophysical sources tested, for example through arrival-direction anisotropy or gamma-ray emission around candidate sources.

**Benchmark.** [1], [2], [6]

## 3. Spatial distribution against the dark matter distribution

**Question.** Does the spatial distribution of the excess follow the expected DM signal (the density squared for annihilation, the density for decay, integrated along the line of sight) and not the distribution of gas, stars or the Galactic disc, and does the paper state which density profile it assumes and how the result changes with that choice?

**Why it matters.** The predicted signal near the centre of a halo depends sensitively on the assumed inner density profile, and small-scale substructure can raise annihilation signals further. Astrophysical emission generally follows gas and stars. For decay, the signal from any object should be proportional to its total DM content in the field of view, so the relative strength between regions and objects is a direct test.

**Resolved by.** Fits that compare a DM template with templates tracing gas and stars; the centroid, symmetry and radial profile of the excess shown; the assumed profile stated and alternatives, cusped and cored, tested; regions of interest defined before the fit for stated profiles; signal amplitudes in different regions or objects compared with the ratio of their J-factors.

**Benchmark.** [1], [3]

## 4. Consistency with other targets and channels

**Question.** Do the DM mass, annihilation cross section or decay lifetime implied by the excess agree with searches in other targets and channels (dwarf galaxies, galaxy clusters, other galaxies, antiparticle cosmic rays, the cosmic microwave background), with the uncertainty in the DM content of each target included?

**Why it matters.** For given particle properties, a DM signal predicts a definite flux from every DM-rich object. Dwarf galaxies have smaller J-factors than the Galactic centre but little astrophysical emission, and null results there, in the cosmic microwave background or in other channels can exclude the parameters an excess requires. The comparison depends on the J-factor uncertainty of each target and on any velocity dependence of annihilation, which changes the relative signals.

**Resolved by.** The implied parameters plotted against published limits from other targets for the same DM model and final state; J-factor uncertainties included, for example as nuisance parameters in a joint likelihood; any velocity dependence or non-minimal model invoked to reconcile the excess with those limits stated together with the predictions it makes; tension stated where it exists.

**Benchmark.** [1], [4], [6]

## 5. Dependence on the instrument

**Question.** Is the excess shown to be independent of instrumental factors (event class or reconstruction version, detector or camera, observing mode or spacecraft orientation, position in instrument coordinates), and is it seen with a second instrument?

**Why it matters.** Fine energy-dependent structure in the effective area is hard to model and can produce or hide a narrow feature, residual charged cosmic rays classified as photons add a background of their own, and the response of some instruments is poorly known in parts of their energy range. A line-like feature that also appears in a control sample where no signal is expected, or that changes with reprocessing or spacecraft orientation, points to an instrumental origin.

**Resolved by.** The excess compared across event classes, reconstruction versions, detectors and observing modes; the cosmic-ray contamination of the photon sample estimated; a control sample with no expected signal, observed with a similar distribution in instrument coordinates, shown to be free of the feature; for an X-ray line in objects at different redshifts, the line energy shown to shift with redshift; agreement between independent instruments.

**Benchmark.** [3], [5]

## 6. Atomic and other astrophysical lines

**Question.** For a claimed spectral line, which known atomic, charge-exchange and instrumental lines lie within the energy resolution, how were their strengths fixed, and are the energy and width of the line consistent with a DM origin in every target?

**Why it matters.** Hot gas in galaxies and clusters emits many atomic lines in the X-ray band, so a weak line can lie within the energy resolution of known lines. Their strengths depend on plasma temperature and abundances, and charge exchange can add emission at similar energies. A line left free, or fixed without reference to measured partner lines, can absorb or create a signal.

**Resolved by.** Nearby lines modelled with strengths tied to measured partner lines of higher emissivity and to the fitted plasma temperature; charge-exchange emission considered and constrained; the continuum model shown to leave residuals much smaller than the line; the line energy consistent between targets; where the energy resolution allows, the line width compared with the Doppler broadening expected from DM motions.

**Benchmark.** [1], [5]

## 7. Cosmic-ray propagation and secondary production

**Question.** For an excess in cosmic-ray positrons, antiprotons or antinuclei, how were the secondary background, Galactic propagation (diffusion coefficient and halo height), production cross sections (including coalescence for antinuclei) and solar modulation modelled, were they constrained by measured secondary-to-primary ratios, and were their uncertainties carried into the significance?

**Why it matters.** Antiparticles are produced mainly as secondaries in collisions of cosmic rays with interstellar gas, and their predicted flux depends on the poorly known halo height and diffusion, on production cross sections, which are especially uncertain for antinuclei, and, at low energy, on transport through the heliosphere. A modest excess can fall within these uncertainties, and alternatives such as secondary production inside the accelerating sources can produce one.

**Resolved by.** Propagation parameters fitted to secondary-to-primary ratios such as boron to carbon; production cross-section uncertainties included; solar modulation modelled and the lowest energies treated with stated caution; the significance shown under alternative propagation set-ups; astrophysical alternatives such as primary sources or production at the source tested against the same data.

**Benchmark.** [1], [6]

## 8. Control regions and global significance

**Question.** Is the significance of the excess calibrated against regions or samples where no DM signal is expected, and is it corrected for the number of masses, final states, regions and targets searched?

**Why it matters.** Regions along the Galactic plane where no DM signal is expected can show DM-like excesses of similar size relative to the background, which shows how large the excesses produced by background-model imperfections alone can be. A local significance at the best mass and region overstates the evidence when many overlapping fits are made, and a feature that is a fluctuation weakens as data accumulate.

**Resolved by.** The size of DM-like features in control regions or blank fields measured and included as a systematic uncertainty; a global significance from background-only simulations of the full search, not from assuming independent fits; the result checked again with additional data.

**Benchmark.** [2], [3], [4]
