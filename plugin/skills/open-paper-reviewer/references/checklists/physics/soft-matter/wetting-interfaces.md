# Wetting, interfaces and surface tension

Contact-angle goniometry (measuring the angle a drop makes with a solid from its silhouette) and drop-shape tensiometry (fitting the shape of a pendant or sessile drop to the Young–Laplace equation) are used to claim how strongly a liquid wets or is repelled by a surface, including superhydrophobicity, and the surface or interfacial tension of a liquid and how surfactants lower it. Both infer a property of an interface from the outline of a drop of finite size, under gravity, in an environment that can change the interface during the measurement.

## 1. Advancing and receding angles

**Question.** Does the paper report advancing and receding contact angles, measured on a drop whose contact line is moving, and say how they were obtained, or does it rest a claim about wettability on a single angle of a drop placed on the surface?

**Why it matters.** On real surfaces the angle of a deposited drop depends on how the drop came to rest and can sit in a metastable state, so a single "static" value does not characterise the surface. Advancing and receding angles, and the hysteresis between them, are set by the contact line and are what govern whether drops spread, pin or roll off.

**Resolved by.** Advancing and receding angles reported separately, with the method named (slowly growing and shrinking the drop, a tilted or moving stage), the rate of volume change or stage speed, and evidence that the contact line actually moved when the receding angle was read; the hysteresis given; where a single angle is reported, a statement of how the drop was deposited and why that angle is enough for the claim.

## 2. Drop size, gravity and contour fitting

**Question.** What drop volume, liquid, optical magnification and fitting method were used to obtain each angle, and is the drop small enough, compared with the capillary length, and imaged finely enough near the contact line for the method to hold?

**Why it matters.** Larger drops are flattened by gravity, which lowers the apparent angle, while very small drops can be distorted by the needle. Simple geometric fits fail once gravity matters, and near the contact line the image can be blurred, so an angle read from too coarse an image can be underestimated by several degrees, more so for liquids with a short capillary length.

**Resolved by.** Drop volume, dispensing method and fitting model stated for each data set; the drop size compared with the capillary length, or a Young–Laplace fit used where gravity matters; images of fitted drops shown; magnification and pixel size given and chosen for the liquid used; angles from both sides of the drop and their difference reported.

**Benchmark.** [3], [4], [5]

## 3. Surface and liquid condition

**Question.** How were the samples prepared, cleaned and stored before measurement, how long were they exposed to air, and what was the purity of the probe liquid?

**Why it matters.** Surfaces adsorb airborne hydrocarbons and pick up material from packaging, and the measured angle can change by tens of percent with storage alone. Cleaning steps alter or remove such layers, and impure water gives different angles on hydrophilic surfaces, so differences between samples can come from handling rather than from the property claimed.

**Resolved by.** Preparation, cleaning and storage described and applied identically to every sample compared; the time between preparation and measurement stated; surface composition checked, for example by photoelectron spectroscopy, where the claim depends on surface chemistry; probe liquid grade and purity given; the measurement repeated after ageing where long-term behaviour is claimed.

**Benchmark.** [3]

## 4. Roughness, heterogeneity and the Wenzel and Cassie models

**Question.** When the paper explains an angle by surface roughness or composition using the Wenzel or Cassie–Baxter relations, does it characterise the surface at the contact line and over the area sampled, and test the interpretation instead of fitting it?

**Why it matters.** Advancing, receding and hysteresis are set by what happens at the three-phase contact line; the structure under the drop does not by itself fix them, so roughness ratios and area fractions averaged over the whole surface can give a misleading account. A goniometer averages over millimetres, so local defects and patchy coatings that control pinning are hidden, and changes in angle with treatment may come from chemistry as well as topography.

**Resolved by.** Roughness and composition measured on the same samples and on a scale relevant to the contact line; advancing and receding angles, not only one angle, compared with the models; measurements at several locations, or a mapping method, showing how uniform the surface is; the roughness and chemical contributions separated, for example with smooth reference surfaces of the same chemistry.

**Benchmark.** [2], [6], [7]

## 5. Claims of superhydrophobicity or liquid repellence

**Question.** For angles above about 150°, does the paper give advancing and receding angles with an uncertainty that reflects the difficulty of locating the baseline, and does it show that the non-wetting state is stable under the conditions of the claimed use?

**Why it matters.** Near 180° the baseline is ambiguous, the image near the contact line is poor and the result depends strongly on drop size, so errors of up to about 10° are possible and averaged values can even exceed 180°. A high apparent angle with a low receding angle still means that drops adhere, and sliding angles show stick–slip motion and wide scatter. The air-trapping state that gives repellence is often metastable and can collapse into the wetted state under pressure, vibration, evaporation or the drop's own weight.

**Resolved by.** Advancing and receding angles with stated uncertainties, the baseline method described and no values that are impossible; the receding angle or hysteresis used for the claim, with sliding angles given with drop volume and their spread; tests of the non-wetting state relevant to the claim, such as pressure, impact, immersion or evaporation; measurements at several positions on the sample.

**Benchmark.** [4], [5], [6], [7]

## 6. Evaporation, humidity and timing

**Question.** Were temperature and humidity controlled or recorded, how long after deposition was each angle read, and does the paper show that evaporation did not change the angle or the drop during the measurement?

**Why it matters.** In a dry atmosphere an evaporating drop can lose a large part of its angle and volume within minutes. Exposure of hydrophilic surfaces to humid air before a measurement can also change the result, and evaporation can trigger a transition out of a non-wetting state.

**Resolved by.** Temperature and relative humidity stated; the time between deposition and reading stated and kept short, or angle and drop volume shown against time; a saturated or enclosed environment used for slow measurements; a check that the reported value does not depend on the waiting time.

**Benchmark.** [3], [7]

## 7. Drop-shape tensiometry

**Question.** For surface or interfacial tension from a pendant or sessile drop, does the paper give the drop volume relative to the largest stable drop, the densities used, the needle size and the fitting procedure, and does it check the setup on a liquid of known tension?

**Why it matters.** The tension is recovered from how gravity deforms the drop, so a nearly spherical drop carries little information and gives an imprecise value; precision falls steeply as the drop volume drops below the volume at detachment. The result is proportional to the density difference, so an error there passes directly into the tension.

**Resolved by.** Drop volume, needle diameter and the density difference with its source stated; a measure of drop deformation such as the Bond or Worthington number reported and large enough for the precision claimed; the fitted profile shown over the image; the instrument checked against a pure reference liquid measured the same way.

**Benchmark.** [8], [9]

## 8. Dynamic versus equilibrium tension

**Question.** When the paper reports an equilibrium surface tension, a critical micelle concentration or an adsorption isotherm, does it show the tension against surface age and state how long each interface was left to equilibrate, and does it consider depletion of surfactant from the drop and the purity of the surfactant?

**Why it matters.** Surfactant solutions approach equilibrium on timescales from milliseconds to many hours, so a value read too early is a dynamic tension and depends on the method and the age of the surface. In a small drop, adsorption can strip surfactant from the bulk so that the equilibrium concentration is far below the nominal one. Traces of a more surface-active impurity lower the tension and can produce a minimum near the critical micelle concentration, so the shape of the curve may reflect purity rather than the surfactant.

**Resolved by.** Tension plotted against surface age for representative concentrations, with the value taken from a plateau or an extrapolation that is stated; a method whose timescale matches the adsorption kinetics; depletion estimated or avoided, for example with larger drops or bubbles in a large reservoir; surfactant source, purity and any purification given; the absence of a minimum near the critical micelle concentration checked or discussed.

**Benchmark.** [9], [10], [11]
