| 1 | doi:10.1007/s00216-010-4221-7 | full text | Introduction and "Separation of branched polymers": SEC separates by hydrodynamic volume, branched and linear chains of different molar mass co-elute, and molar masses are local averages. "Important prerequisites": apparent molar masses from linear standards give significant non-systematic errors for branched polymers; complete dissolution is essential and heat, sonication or extreme pH can degrade chains; ultrahigh-molar-mass polymers can be degraded by shear during separation, and excessive injection concentration causes shear degradation and band broadening. Conclusion: validate molar masses with two independent methods |
| 2 | doi:10.3390/polym17050582 | full text | Sec. 2.1: conventional calibration with standards such as polystyrene gives molar masses relative to the standards; universal calibration through hydrodynamic volume and the intrinsic viscosity. Secs. 2.2 and 3: light scattering gives molar mass through an optical constant containing the refractive index increment, which is also needed for the concentration from the refractive-index detector. Secs. 4.1.3 and 5.4: definitions of the number-, weight- and z-average molar masses; Mn and Mz are less reliable than Mw because they come from the ends of the elution range, where detector signal-to-noise is poor |
| 3 | doi:10.1007/s13367-024-00111-3 | abstract | Abstract (Semantic Scholar record): molecular weight reduction by chain scission is a common degradation mechanism; time sweep tests monitor stability, frequency sweeps reveal effects of processing and thermal history, and higher-shear tests that simulate processing show that shear accelerates degradation |
| 4 | doi:10.1017/jfm.2020.1092 | abstract | Abstract: turbulence stretches and breaks polymers; the effect of polymers on the flow is transient because it disappears as they break; fragments can break again, giving a population of chains with a range of relaxation times |
| 5 | doi:10.1016/j.cocis.2019.03.001 | full text | Sec. 2.1: the solvent-quality parameter combines the distance from the theta temperature with the square root of chain length; chains follow random-walk statistics at theta and self-avoiding statistics in athermal solvent, with a crossover between; universal predictions hold only in the long-chain limit. Sec. 2.2: definition of c* and the scaling of properties with c/c*. Sec. 2.3: in the semidilute regime at intermediate solvent quality, properties follow an effective exponent that depends on solvent quality, and viscosity data collapse when plotted against both variables |
| 6 | doi:10.1122/1.2357595 | abstract | Abstract (MIT group publication page): between about 0.01 and 1 times c* the relaxation time in extensional flow is much larger than the value from oscillatory shear or Zimm theory, and its power-law dependence on c/c* has an exponent that varies with solvent quality; a minimum concentration below which viscoelasticity cannot be resolved |
| 7 | doi:10.1016/S0032-3861(02)00235-5 | abstract | Abstract (University of Birmingham repository record): the main difficulty in DSC crystallinity is that the sample recrystallises while it melts on heating, and enthalpies and crystallinity change with temperature; an arbitrary choice of the liquid heat capacity introduces systematic errors; DSC results compared with density |
| 8 | doi:10.1002/adem.201900796 | abstract | Abstract (university research record): the enthalpy of fusion cannot be determined directly by DSC and must be calibrated by external methods such as X-ray diffraction or density; literature values for polypropylene range from about 60 to 260 J/g; crystallinity varied widely with cooling rates between 1 and about 3500 K/min |
| 9 | doi:10.1007/s00397-025-01526-7 | abstract | Abstract: four gel criteria compared (crossover of the moduli, frequency-independent loss tangent, loss-tangent peak, divergence of relaxation time); a physical filler network can mask the usual gel indicators; many composites have a narrow linear viscoelastic range; with a weak filler network the frequency-independent loss tangent best matched the neat resin gel point, and with a strong network the loss tangent was not frequency independent |
