| 1 | doi:10.1038/nature23458 | full text | "Requirements for quantum supremacy": a well-defined task, a quantum algorithm, a time and space budget for the classical competitor, a complexity assumption and optionally verification. "Fine-grained complexity assumptions": asymptotic statements say little about the devices being built, and a claim about a specific experiment needs concrete cost bounds at that size. "Maximal assumptions": hardness conjectures are subject to revision as new algorithms appear. "Physical noise and simulation errors": excessive noise can make a hard distribution easy to simulate. "Verification": all known verification methods have drawbacks; tests that need individual output probabilities are hard to compute classically, and a post-selecting classical simulator could cheat some tests. "Outlook": expects better classical simulations to challenge proposed experiments |

Secs. III and V.1: time to solution counts the repetitions needed for a target success probability, and wall-clock time adds programming and readout. Sec. IV.2: classical hardware should scale with problem size as the quantum hardware does. Sec. V.4.1: optimised classical codes on full processors, not generic single-core codes. Secs.
